# Senate primary model: champion case and limits

Advisory to the primary evaluator; this does not replace the forecast or authorize a trade.

## Strongest case for using the model

The model is decision-useful as an explicit map from state margins to the exact Senate-control question, not as a calibrated election forecaster. Its seat arithmetic is corrected: 33 regular contests (13 D-held and 20 R-held) plus Ohio and Florida special elections (both R-held) give 35 contests; the other 65 seats start at 34 D-aligned and 31 R. The literal market rule also matters: a party needs a majority of voting seats; at 50–50, control goes to the party holding the Vice Presidency; only unresolved cases reach the first-selected President Pro Tempore. The model represents the tie and independent-caucus branches rather than treating a popular-vote margin as Senate control. See the exact frozen text in `research/2026-10-17-primary-market-review-rules.jsonl.gz` (review index 52) and the arithmetic correction in [senate_specials_independent_correction.json](../../../data/deep_review_20261017/senate_specials_independent_correction.json).

Its strongest empirical foundation is the consistent same-source 2024 state presidential baseline, joined to dated candidate polls in 11 contests. The code makes the bridge inspectable: a stated generic-ballot environment and national-swing transfer form a structural prior; poll weights reflect reported precision, age and source-specific error; and a candidate-share margin of error is doubled before using it for a D–R margin. A shared poll-source floor and a single common poll-bias draw keep several releases from masquerading as independent confirmation. Recent primary poll evidence can move the state centers while leaving explicit residual and national uncertainty. The saved inputs, code and result scenarios make each assumption reproducible, which is more useful for sizing a small position than either a headline or a point forecast with hidden inputs.

The current draft’s central output is 51.5% D control, with explicit alternatives rather than a false precision claim. The most favorable supporting sensitivity is not a new estimate: removing the assumed −1.5-point mean poll correction gives 60.5%; the adverse −3-point case gives 42.7%. The candidate-transfer, environment, NE caucus, and shared-error dimensions are exposed separately. For a compound D-Senate/D-House contract, the model uses correlated draws while preserving the existing House marginal near 80%; the draft gives 47.9% joint D/D, versus about 41.3% under independence from those marginals. That is a meaningful framework for comparing a very small maker entry with its exact fee-adjusted price, provided the primary uses a price below a suitably conservative value and preserves ordinary risk limits. The 0.35–0.85 House-loading sensitivity moves D/D from 44.5% to 49.4% while leaving the Senate marginal unchanged.

## Evidence that should stay in view

The 51.5% central result is a draft sensitivity, not calibrated odds. The result is close to a coin flip and depends materially on judgmental inputs: changing the generic D margin from +8 to +6/+10 moves D control to 43.6%/59.7%; removing/fully retaining historical personal effects moves it to 43.3%/54.3%. The −1.5-point shared poll correction alone shifts the result by about nine points relative to a zero-mean correction. Those choices are transparent and can be stress-tested, but there is no hindcast showing that these coefficients predict Senate control reliably. The run uses 60,000 Monte Carlo draws, which reduce simulation noise but do not reduce uncertainty in the assumptions.

Candidate-transfer assumptions deserve special skepticism. The 13.8-point Osborn overperformance is same-candidate/state context against a different opponent and cycle; Mary Peltola’s 11.1-point comparison comes from a House contest, then transfers to a Senate race; Sherrod Brown’s 7.6-point value blends incumbent-specific strength with the 2024 environment. Retaining 75% of these values is a subjective bridge, not an identified personal vote. The 0–100% retention sensitivity is therefore more informative than the central coefficient. Similar caution applies to a single assumed common national poll bias: it usefully avoids overcounting correlated polls, but its mean and variance are not estimated from this selected 2026 poll set.

The exact rule makes Nebraska a discrete settlement fork, not a generic “independent helps Democrats” adjustment. Osborn is an independent, not a Democratic nominee; his reported “caucus with everybody” phrasing is not an announced party caucus. The market can remain open for a newly elected independent’s intent until the earlier announcement or January 4 deadline. The model’s future D/R/neither caucus probabilities and 50% Democratic Pro Tem branch are judgmental. If a 50–49–1 result leaves no party with 50 plus the Republican Vice President, actual first-selected Pro Tem affiliation can determine control and timing. This is both outcome risk and capital-duration risk. CTM’s 52.6% strict D-control comparison (without NE alignment) and 56% presumed-alignment figure are useful cross-checks only; neither is an independent truth or a target for fitting the primary model.

Most importantly, the currently saved draft has no Florida or South Carolina poll inputs. Florida is one of the two special elections, and two fresh original releases now name Angie Nixon—not Alex Vindman. MDW/EDGE reports Moody 47.6–Nixon 42.4 (Oct. 4–10; n=2,351 likely voters); Stetson CPOR reports 52–41 (Oct. 1–9; 716 registered voters in a non-probability panel; ±4.2 design-effect-adjusted precision, not a conventional sampling margin). Their difference and methods need explicit weighting, not omission or naive averaging. South Carolina’s current original poll and nominee/ballot evidence remain unverified; the reported Graham 46–44 aggregator topline is not an original poll. See [the bounded FL/SC packet](../../../data/deep_review_20261017/senate_secondary_battlegrounds_fl_sc.json). Until the primary incorporates Florida and decides how to handle the South Carolina gap, the draft central output should not be presented as complete.

Finally, the exact market includes all qualifying November regular and special elections scheduled as of October 31, including qualifying runoffs; later seat changes for non-contested seats are treated under separate rule clauses. A new qualifying special, nominee/ballot change, unresolved caucus announcement, or delayed Pro Tem selection can alter the count or settlement horizon. For Senate/House joint positions, common election environment and exposure overlap mean the contracts are not independent; the model’s correlation is a useful starting point, not a learned joint calibration. A tiny favorable-price entry is defensible only conditionally—after source coverage is repaired, exact current books/fees and rule status are checked, and the offered price remains attractive under primary-owned stressed inputs. The model itself supplies no price edge because venue prices are deliberately excluded.
