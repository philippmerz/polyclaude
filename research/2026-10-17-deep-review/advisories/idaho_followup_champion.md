# Independent champion — Idaho Senate case 1870

The best affirmative case is that the revised model now gives the newest, more election-like poll the dominant role without erasing a genuinely conflicting, non-candidate-commissioned result. The frozen claim is narrowly whether Todd Achilles wins Idaho’s 2026 U.S. Senate election, including any runoff; if he wins as an Independent, the rule expressly does not turn that into a Democratic or Republican win. This can therefore have value as a standalone candidate-outcome claim, but it is not a clean Senate-control hedge.

The primary’s revised 25% ATR / 75% Peak mixture is directionally defensible. Peak’s Sep. 28–30 poll is now supported by a pollster-branded memo image: 500 likely 2026 voters, live phone, Risch 49 / Achilles 24 / Fleming 3 / Loesby 2 / none 6 / don’t know 15, ±4 share-point MOE. The later field date, likely-voter screen, and fuller named ballot make it the more relevant central regime. ATR’s Sep. 13–16 release remains meaningful counterevidence rather than something to discard: 700 registered voters, 87.8% text / 12.2% live interviews, explicitly not commissioned by a candidate, with Achilles 45.5 / Risch 39.4 / Loesby 4.5 / unsure 10.6 on its published three-way test. The sources do not establish a controlled trend: screens, modes, dates, candidate lists and question context differ. Peak’s incumbent-campaign sponsorship is attributed by the reporter’s post, while the displayed memo itself names Peak Insights; the full Peak questionnaire, weighting and response rate remain unavailable.

Using the primary’s saved simulation as an attributed sensitivity—not an independent forecast—the revised 25/75 specification yields about 19.9% Achilles win, versus 74.2% if ATR alone governs and 1.7% if Peak alone governs; 10% ATR gives 9.0%, and 40% gives 30.5%. That wide regime spread shows why the principal uncertainty is which instrument better represents the actual electorate, not Monte Carlo noise. Weighting Peak more heavily is a reasonable update to its stronger screen and fuller slate, but 25/75 is still a judgment rather than a likelihood-calibrated combination. Retaining ATR leaves a concrete route to material upside if the registered-voter result better captures turnout or if the incumbent-sponsored live-phone estimate is less representative than its likely-voter label suggests. Those are plausible mechanisms, not demonstrated corrections to Peak.

The reported 20% central estimate can support a positive expected-value thesis only at a price that pays for this model risk and the 15-point reserve; no live quote was reviewed here, so I cannot establish that the current entry has independent edge. The case is weaker as Senate-control exposure: the campaign-hosted Sep. 21 interview page headline says Achilles would caucus with neither party, and the model assigns 90% to neither, with 5% each to future D or R caucus as reversal judgments. The frozen rule also makes an Independent’s election party-neutral regardless of affiliation. Do not credit a win to D control absent a qualifying caucus statement.

Before relying on the central estimate, verify the official final Idaho ballot and whether Achilles, Fleming, Loesby and any other qualified candidate appear on it; resolve the remaining ballot gap from the official portal/sample ballot. Obtain Peak’s actual questionnaire and any weighting/candidate-order details if available, and compare its full ballot wording to ATR’s (including whether ATR respondents saw a biography or sequence effect—the release does not say). Confirm whether the reported MOEs and any nonresponse adjustments are comparable. Test a direct ATR/Peak regime grid and a wider residual for the unresolved instrument differences; do not describe the two reports as repeated measurements of one question. Keep the no-caucus evidence distinct from the Senate-control settlement branch. These checks could materially change the named-candidate estimate; neither present poll identifies the eventual outcome with high precision.
